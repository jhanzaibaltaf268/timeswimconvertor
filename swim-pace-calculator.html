What swim pace is

Pace is how long a fixed chunk of distance takes you. In swimming that chunk is almost always 100 — 100 metres or 100 yards — so a pace of 1:30 per 100 means that, on average, every hundred of your swim took a minute and a half.

It is the number coaches set sets from, the number you pace a distance race by, and the only fair way to compare a 400 with a 1500. A time on its own tells you how long you were in the water. A pace tells you how fast you were actually moving.

Why pace is quoted per 100

Because 100 is the common denominator of almost every pool distance. A 25-metre pool is four lengths to a hundred, a 50-metre pool is two, and every standard race from the 200 upwards divides cleanly by it. Quote a pace per 100 and any swimmer can place it immediately.

Shorter benchmarks exist — per 50 for sprinters, per 25 for stroke work — and the calculator above gives you both. But per 100 is the one written on the whiteboard.

Pace versus speed

They are the same information upside down. Pace counts time per distance and gets better as it falls. Speed counts distance per time and gets better as it rises.

pace per 100 = total time ÷ (distance ÷ 100)

speed in m/s = distance ÷ total time

Swimmers think in pace because sets are written in pace. Triathletes and open water swimmers often think in speed, because it compares directly with the bike and the run. The calculator shows both so you do not have to pick.

How to calculate swimming pace

Three steps, and you can do it on the pool deck.

  • Turn the time into seconds. A 6:12 400 is 372 seconds.
  • Divide the distance by 100. A 400 is four hundreds.
  • Divide one by the other. 372 ÷ 4 = 93 seconds, which is 1:33 per 100.

For a yards pool the arithmetic is identical — you just end up with a pace per 100 yards, which is not the same thing as a pace per 100 metres. A 100-yard pace looks about nine per cent faster than the same effort in metres, because the distance is shorter.

Critical swim speed calculator (CSS test)

Critical swim speed, usually shortened to CSS, is an estimate of the fastest pace you can hold without fatigue running away from you. It is the swimming equivalent of a runner's threshold pace, and it is the number most distance training is built around.

You find it with two all-out time trials, a 400 and a 200, swum fresh with a proper rest between them. The gap between the two tells you how much speed you lose as distance grows, and that slope is your sustainable pace.

CSS pace per 100 = (400 − 200) ÷ (T400 − T200) … inverted to time per 100

In practice: CSS per 100 = (T400 − T200) ÷ 2, where both times are in seconds.

Training at CSS

Once you have the number, most threshold sets sit right on it or a second or two either side. Long reps — 200s, 300s, 400s — with short rest, held at CSS pace, are the classic way to raise it. Sprint work sits well below it; easy aerobic swimming sits well above.

Retest every six to eight weeks. If your CSS pace drops, your endurance has improved even if your race times have not caught up yet.

Why your pace slows over distance

Nobody holds their 100 pace for a 1500. The drop is steep at first and then flattens: most swimmers lose a lot between the 100 and the 400, and comparatively little between the 800 and the 1500.

That shape is why a single pace number is only meaningful next to the distance it came from. A 1:20 per 100 taken from a 100 time trial and a 1:20 taken from a 1500 describe two completely different swimmers. When you compare paces, compare them over the same distance.

A quick sense check: if your 400 pace is more than about ten seconds per 100 slower than your 100 pace, your aerobic base is the limiter. If the two are close, your top-end speed is.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate swim pace?

Divide your total time in seconds by the number of hundreds in the distance. A 6:00 400 is 360 seconds over four hundreds, which is 90 seconds, or 1:30 per 100. The calculator above does it for any distance, in metres or yards.

What is a good or average swim pace per 100m?

It depends entirely on the distance and the swimmer. As a rough guide for freestyle over a sustained swim, around 2:00 per 100 m is a comfortable lap swimmer, 1:40 is a solid club swimmer, 1:25 is competitive masters, and under 1:10 is national level. Compare yourself against the same distance, not against a sprint pace.

What is critical swim speed?

CSS is the fastest pace you can sustain aerobically, estimated from an all-out 400 and an all-out 200. Subtract the 200 time from the 400 time and divide by two — that is your CSS pace per 100. It is swimming's version of threshold pace.

Is pace per 100 yards the same as per 100 metres?

No. A hundred yards is 91.44 metres, so a yards pace is covering about nine per cent less water. A 1:30 per 100 yards is roughly a 1:38 per 100 metres for the same effort. Always say which unit your pace is in.

How do I turn my pace into speed?

Divide 100 by your pace in seconds to get metres per second. A 1:30 per 100 m is 100 ÷ 90 = 1.11 m/s, which is 4.0 km/h. The calculator shows both alongside your pace.

How much can swim pace improve?

Newer swimmers often take ten or more seconds per 100 off within a season, because most of the gain comes from technique rather than fitness. Trained swimmers measure progress in one or two seconds per 100 over the same period. Retest with the same distance and the same rest each time.

How do I use a swim pace chart?

Find your pace per 100 in the first column and read across to the distance you want. The figure is the finish time if you hold that pace the whole way. Use the metres chart for 25 m and 50 m pools and the yards chart for 25-yard pools.

Can I use this swim pace calculator for triathlon or open water?

Yes. Choose a custom distance, such as 750, 1900 or 3800 metres, and enter your target or finished time. In open water, conditions and the line you swim change the result, so compare open water swims with care.
